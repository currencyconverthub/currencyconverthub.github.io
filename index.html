How Currency Conversion Works

Every currency conversion — whether you are changing holiday money, paying an overseas invoice or sending funds to family abroad — follows the same basic mechanics. Understanding them takes five minutes and can save you real money for the rest of your life.

It starts with an exchange rate

An exchange rate is simply a price: how much of one currency you must give up to receive one unit of another. When you read that 1 US dollar equals 0.92 euros, the dollar is the base currency and the euro is the quote currency. Converting is then plain arithmetic — multiply your amount by the rate.

250 USD × 0.92 = 230 EUR

That is the whole calculation. Everything else in the world of currency conversion — the fees, the margins, the different prices at different providers — is about which rate gets used in that multiplication, and what gets taken off the top before the money reaches you.

Where exchange rates come from

Currencies trade on the global foreign exchange market, the largest financial market in the world, where banks, governments, funds and large companies buy and sell currencies around the clock. The price moves constantly in response to interest rates, inflation, trade figures, political events and plain supply and demand. There is no single official price set by a committee; there is a market price that emerges from millions of trades.

The rate shown on CurrencyConverterHub is the mid-market rate: the midpoint between the highest price buyers are offering and the lowest price sellers are accepting at that moment. It is the cleanest reference point available, because no single provider's profit margin is baked into it.

Cross rates: converting between two non-dollar currencies

Most published rates are quoted against the US dollar, because the dollar sits on one side of the majority of the world's currency trades. To convert between two other currencies — say, British pounds to Pakistani rupees — the market works through the dollar in two steps: pounds to dollars, then dollars to rupees. This is called a cross rate, and it is exactly what our converter does behind the scenes:

GBP → PKR rate = (USD per GBP) × (PKR per USD)

The result is mathematically identical to trading the pair directly, and it means a converter with dollar-based data can still price any pair of currencies accurately.

Why the amount you receive is less than the calculator says

If the mid-market calculation says your 250 dollars should become 230 euros, a bank might hand you 224. Three things eat the difference:

  • The spread. Providers buy currency at one price and sell it at a slightly worse one. The gap between their buy and sell prices is profit, and it is charged invisibly inside the rate itself.
  • Fixed fees. Many providers add a visible charge per transfer — a few dollars for an online service, considerably more for an international wire at a traditional bank.
  • Correspondent charges. International bank transfers pass through intermediary banks, and each one may deduct a fee before the money arrives.

None of this is necessarily unfair — moving money across borders costs money to do safely. The problem is only that the biggest cost, the spread, is usually hidden. Comparing any quote against the mid-market rate on this page makes it visible in seconds.

Fixed, floating and pegged currencies

Most major currencies float freely: their price is set entirely by the market. Some governments instead peg their currency to another — the Hong Kong dollar tracks the US dollar within a tight band, for example — and a few fix the rate by law. Pegged currencies move in step with their anchor, which is why their rates against the dollar barely change from day to day while their rates against everything else move exactly as the anchor does.

A practical routine for converting money

  • Check the mid-market rate here first, so you know the fair value of your money.
  • Get quotes from two or three providers and compare the final amount you would receive — not the fee alone, and not the rate alone.
  • Watch out for "zero commission" offers; the cost is almost always inside the rate instead.
  • For large amounts, remember rates move. A 1% swing on a house deposit is serious money, so convert close to the day you need the funds.

The one-sentence version: conversion is just multiplication — your amount times an exchange rate — and the only real question is how far the rate you are offered sits from the mid-market rate shown on this site.

Understanding Exchange Rates: Mid-Market, Buy and Sell Rates

Ask three different providers what a euro costs and you will get three different answers — all of them honest, all of them different. That is because a currency never has one price. It has at least three, and knowing which is which is the key to never overpaying for an exchange again.

The buy rate and the sell rate

Every currency dealer quotes two prices at the same time. The buy rate (also called the bid) is the price at which the dealer will buy a currency from you. The sell rate (the ask or offer) is the price at which the dealer will sell that currency to you. The sell rate is always less favourable to you than the buy rate — the dealer sells dear and buys cheap, exactly like any other merchant.

Suppose a bureau quotes the euro at 0.90 to buy and 0.96 to sell, per US dollar's worth of trade. Hand over 100 euros and you receive about 90 dollars. Immediately buy those euros back and you would need about 96 dollars. Nothing about the market moved; the six-dollar difference is the dealer's margin, called the spread.

The mid-market rate sits exactly in between

Take the average of the buy and sell prices and you get the mid-market rate — 0.93 in the example above. This is the rate quoted by news services, search engines and reference converters like this one. No customer actually trades at the mid-market rate; it exists as a neutral benchmark, the "true" market price before any individual provider adds its costs.

Think of the mid-market rate as the wholesale price of currency, and the buy and sell rates as retail prices. The further a retail quote sits from wholesale, the more the exchange is costing you.

How wide is a normal spread?

It depends heavily on where you exchange and what you exchange:

  • Major pairs, online specialists (USD/EUR, GBP/USD): often well under 1% from mid-market.
  • Traditional banks: commonly 1% to 3%, sometimes more for less common currencies.
  • Airport and hotel bureaus: frequently 5% to 10% or worse — the most expensive place to change money.
  • Less traded currencies: wider spreads everywhere, because dealers hold them less and price in more risk.

On a small holiday exchange, a 3% spread costs a few dollars. On a 20,000-dollar transfer, it costs 600 — which is why the spread matters far more than any advertised fee.

"No commission" does not mean free

A bureau can truthfully advertise zero commission while quoting a sell rate 8% away from mid-market. The commission has not disappeared; it has moved into the rate, where most customers never think to look. The only reliable test of any offer is to compare the final amount you receive against the mid-market calculation for the same amount, on the same day.

Which rate should you use for planning?

Use the mid-market rate — the one this site shows — as your reference for budgeting, invoicing and comparing quotes. Then expect the amount you actually receive to be a little lower, and treat anything within about 1% of mid-market on a major currency pair as a fair retail deal. If a quote is 4% or more away, shop around: on any significant sum, a few minutes of comparison is the best-paid work you will do that day.

Best Time to Exchange Currency: Does Timing Really Matter?

Everyone who has watched an exchange rate for a few days has had the same thought: if I had exchanged last Tuesday, I would have got more. So is there a best time to exchange currency — and can an ordinary person actually use it? The honest answer is "sometimes, a little" — and the details matter more than the timing.

First, the uncomfortable truth about predicting rates

Exchange rates are set by a market where professional traders, central banks and algorithms move trillions of dollars every day. If the future direction of a currency were reliably predictable, those professionals would already have traded on it — and their trading would move the price until the opportunity vanished. This is why decades of research keep reaching the same conclusion: short-term currency movements are close to random, and no app, chart pattern or tipster can tell you next week's rate with dependable accuracy.

That does not make timing pointless. It means timing should be treated as risk management, not as a way to outsmart the market. You cannot reliably buy at the bottom, but you can avoid exchanging at obviously bad moments, and you can structure a large conversion so that one unlucky day does not decide the whole outcome.

What actually moves an exchange rate

Understanding the forces behind a rate helps you recognise the moments when a move is likely to be sharp — even if you cannot know its direction in advance:

  • Interest rate decisions. When a central bank raises rates, holding that currency pays more, and demand for it usually rises. Rate announcements are scheduled, so the risky moments are known in advance.
  • Inflation and jobs data. Monthly figures that surprise the market can move a major currency by half a percent or more within minutes.
  • Politics and crises. Elections, trade disputes and conflicts push money toward "safe haven" currencies such as the US dollar, Swiss franc and Japanese yen.
  • Long-term trends. Over months and years, rates follow economic fundamentals — growth, inflation, government debt and trade balances. Trends are real, but they reverse without asking permission.

The weekly rhythm: days and hours

Currency markets open on Monday morning in Asia and close on Friday evening in New York. Within that week, a few patterns are worth knowing:

  • Weekends are frozen. Rates barely move from Friday's close to Monday's open, so the rate you see on Saturday is really Friday's rate. Any weekend news lands all at once on Monday, which is why Monday opens can jump.
  • Midweek is the most liquid. Tuesday to Thursday, when all major financial centres are active, spreads are tightest and prices are smoothest. That makes midweek the calmest time to exchange — not because rates are better, but because they are less jumpy.
  • Avoid exchanging during major announcements if you can. In the minutes around a central bank decision, spreads widen dramatically at many providers, so even a "good" rate can come with a bad spread.

When timing genuinely matters: large amounts

Do the arithmetic and the picture becomes clear. On a 300-dollar holiday exchange, a 1% rate movement is worth 3 dollars — far less than the difference between a good provider and a bad one, which can easily be 5% or more. Chasing timing on small amounts is effort spent in the wrong place.

On a 50,000-dollar conversion — a house deposit, a business payment, a year of overseas tuition — that same 1% is 500 dollars, and a 3% swing across a month is 1,500. At that scale, timing deserves real attention, and these are the strategies professionals actually use:

  • Split the conversion. Exchange in three or four tranches over several weeks. You will get the average rate rather than one day's rate, which removes the risk of picking the single worst day.
  • Set a target rate and a deadline. Decide in advance: "I will exchange if the rate reaches X, and in any case by date Y." This replaces emotion with a plan and prevents the endless waiting that ends in a forced exchange at whatever rate the deadline brings.
  • Use rate alerts. Most converter and transfer apps let you set an alert for a rate level. It costs nothing and means you only pay attention when it matters.
  • Start early. The worst exchanges happen under time pressure. If you know a payment is due in three months, you have options; if it is due on Friday, you have none.

The factor that beats timing every time

Compare two travellers exchanging 1,000 dollars. One waits a week for a rate that improves by 0.4% and gains 4 dollars — then exchanges at an airport bureau with an 8% margin and loses 80. The other exchanges immediately through a provider priced within 0.5% of the mid-market rate. Timing was never the main event; the provider's margin was. Our guide to mid-market, buy and sell rates shows how to measure that margin in seconds, and it is worth more than any timing strategy for typical amounts.

The bottom line: you cannot predict exchange rates, and for everyday amounts you do not need to. Exchange midweek when markets are calm, avoid deadline pressure, split very large conversions into tranches — and spend the rest of your energy on the spread, where the real money is lost and saved.

Currency Conversion Fees Explained: How to Pay Less When You Travel

Ask a traveller what their currency exchange cost and they will usually name the fee they saw — a few dollars at the bureau, maybe a charge on a card statement. The uncomfortable truth is that the visible fee is often the smallest part. This article walks through every way a conversion takes its cut, and how to keep each one as small as possible.

Cost number one: the exchange rate margin

The largest cost in most conversions never appears as a fee at all. Providers buy currency at the mid-market rate — the benchmark shown on this site — and sell it to you at a worse one. The difference is the margin, or spread, and it is charged invisibly inside the rate itself.

Mid-market rate: 1 USD = 0.92 EUR Bureau's rate: 1 USD = 0.87 EUR Hidden cost on 500 USD: about 25 EUR — with a sign saying "0% commission"

Margins vary enormously. Online specialists on major currency pairs often work within 0.5% to 1% of mid-market. High-street banks commonly take 2% to 4%. Airport and hotel bureaus can take 8% or more. Because the margin hides in the rate, the only way to compare providers honestly is to compare the final amount you receive for the same amount sent — never the advertised fee, and never the word "free".

Cost number two: explicit fees

On top of the margin, many providers charge visible fees:

  • Flat transfer fees. A fixed charge per transaction — a few dollars from an online service, but potentially 25 to 45 dollars for an international wire at a traditional bank.
  • Percentage fees. Some services charge a percentage of the amount sent, sometimes combined with a smaller margin in the rate. A low margin plus a percentage fee can still beat a "no fee" offer with a fat margin — always compare totals.
  • Intermediary bank charges. International wires pass through correspondent banks, and each may deduct a fee in transit, so the recipient gets less than the sender sent even when the sending bank charged nothing extra.
  • Delivery and cash pick-up fees. Sending to cash pick-up or a mobile wallet sometimes costs more than sending to a bank account.

Cost number three: dynamic currency conversion (DCC)

You are abroad, you pay by card, and the terminal asks: pay in your home currency or the local one? Choosing your home currency feels safer — you can see the amount in familiar money — but it is one of the most expensive buttons in travel. This is dynamic currency conversion: the merchant's payment company converts the bill at a rate it chooses, typically 3% to 8% worse than mid-market, and shares the profit with the merchant.

The rule is simple: always choose to pay in the local currency of the country you are in. Your own bank or card network will then do the conversion, almost always at a far better rate. The same rule applies at foreign ATMs that offer to "convert for you" — decline the conversion and let your bank handle it.

Cost number four: card foreign transaction fees

Many bank cards add a foreign transaction fee — commonly 1% to 3% — on every purchase made in another currency, on top of the card network's conversion rate (which is usually close to mid-market). On a two-week trip spending 2,000 dollars, a 3% fee is 60 dollars spent on nothing. Before travelling, check your card's terms: plenty of travel-oriented cards charge no foreign transaction fee at all, and using one is the single easiest saving most travellers can make. The same check applies to ATM withdrawals abroad, where some banks add both a percentage fee and a fixed charge per withdrawal — making a few larger withdrawals cheaper than many small ones.

Cash, cards and apps: a practical pecking order

  • Best for most spending: a card with no foreign transaction fee, always paying in the local currency. Near mid-market rates, no cash risk.
  • Best for cash: withdraw from local bank ATMs after arrival, declining the ATM's own conversion offer. Avoid exchanging cash at airports and hotels except in an emergency.
  • Best for sending money abroad: a specialist transfer service compared on the total received — rate and fee together — against the mid-market benchmark.
  • Worst almost everywhere: airport bureaus, hotel desks, and any offer that leads with "zero commission".

A five-minute pre-trip checklist

  • Check every card you plan to carry for foreign transaction fees; leave the expensive ones at home.
  • Memorise the DCC rule: local currency, every time, on terminals and ATMs alike.
  • Look up the mid-market rate for your destination on this converter before you go, so you can recognise a bad quote on sight.
  • Tell your bank where you are travelling, so legitimate foreign transactions are not blocked.
  • Carry a small amount of local cash for arrival — bought at a reasonable rate, not an airport one — and a backup card stored separately.

None of these steps requires predicting exchange rates or opening new accounts weeks in advance. Conversion costs are mostly a matter of knowing where they hide — the margin in the rate, the conversion button on the terminal, the percentage buried in your card's terms. Now that you know, you will pay a fraction of what the average traveller pays.

USD to PKR: How the Dollar to Pakistani Rupee Rate Is Set

Anyone who sends money to Pakistan, prices imports in dollars, or simply watches the news has seen the headline: "the rupee gained" or "the rupee fell". But who actually decides how many rupees one dollar is worth? There is no single price-setter — there is a market, a central bank watching over it, and a set of forces that push the rate every working day.

A managed float, not a fixed price

The Pakistani rupee operates under what economists call a managed float. The rate is determined by supply and demand for dollars in Pakistan's foreign exchange market — it is not fixed by law at a set number — but the State Bank of Pakistan (SBP), the country's central bank, monitors the market and can intervene when movements become disorderly. In practice this means the rate moves daily with market conditions, while the central bank acts as a referee with deep pockets: it can buy dollars to slow a sharp rise in the rate, or sell dollars from its reserves to slow a sharp fall.

This arrangement replaced earlier, more tightly controlled regimes. The modern framework — formally adopted in 2019 as a market-based exchange rate system — was designed so that the rate adjusts gradually to economic conditions instead of being held still for long periods and then jumping in sudden devaluations, as happened repeatedly in Pakistan's past.

The interbank market: where the headline rate is made

The USD/PKR rate quoted in the news and shown on converters like this one comes from the interbank market, where commercial banks trade foreign currency with each other and with the central bank. Importers buy dollars through their banks to pay foreign suppliers; exporters sell the dollars they earn; the balance of those flows, minute by minute, sets the rate.

The State Bank publishes reference rates based on this market, and banks price their customer transactions around it — adding a margin, exactly as dealers do everywhere (see our guide to buy and sell rates). When you transfer money to a Pakistani bank account through a formal channel, the conversion happens at a rate derived from the interbank market.

Why there is also an "open market" rate

Alongside the interbank market, Pakistan has an open market: licensed exchange companies and currency shops that buy and sell physical cash and handle walk-in remittances. Their rates are close to the interbank rate but rarely identical, because they deal in cash, serve different customers, and adjust for their own supply of notes.

When the two rates drift far apart, it signals stress — typically that dollars are scarce in the formal system or that demand is leaking into informal channels. In calm periods the gap is small; during the currency pressure of 2022–23 it widened dramatically, and narrowing it again became an explicit policy goal. If you are comparing rates, make sure you know which market a quote comes from: an open-market cash rate and an interbank transfer rate are cousins, not twins.

The forces that push the rupee

  • Workers' remittances. Pakistanis working abroad — especially in Saudi Arabia, the UAE, the UK and the US — send home tens of billions of dollars a year. This is one of the largest sources of dollar supply, and strong remittance months directly support the rupee.
  • Exports and imports. Pakistan exports textiles, rice and other goods, earning dollars; it imports oil, machinery and food, spending them. Because imports usually exceed exports, there is structural demand for dollars that remittances and other inflows must cover.
  • Foreign reserves. The State Bank's reserve level is the market's confidence gauge. Rising reserves calm the rate; falling reserves make traders nervous and can feed speculation against the rupee.
  • IMF programmes and loans. Agreements with the International Monetary Fund, plus loans and deposits from friendly countries, bring in dollars and carry conditions — including keeping the exchange rate market-based. News about these programmes routinely moves the rate.
  • Interest rates and inflation. High domestic interest rates make holding rupees more attractive; high inflation does the opposite, eroding the currency's value over time.

What this means when you send money to Pakistan

For someone converting dollars to rupees, the practical lessons are these. First, the rate you are quoted by any provider should be compared against the interbank-based mid-market rate — the one shown by this converter — because that is the benchmark the whole formal system prices from. Second, expect the rate to move: a managed float still floats, and a difference of a rupee or two per dollar between Monday and Friday is normal market weather, not a sign anyone is cheating you. Third, the gap between providers is usually worth more than the gap between days — on a 500-dollar remittance, a provider 1% closer to mid-market puts roughly five dollars' more rupees in the recipient's hands, every single time.

In one sentence: the dollar–rupee rate is set by supply and demand in the interbank market — driven by remittances, trade and reserves — with the State Bank of Pakistan smoothing the extremes rather than fixing the price.

Fixed vs Floating Exchange Rates: What a Currency Peg Means

Check the US dollar against the Saudi riyal today, next month, or five years from now, and you will find essentially the same number. Check it against the Japanese yen and it will have moved by the time you finish this article. The difference is not an accident of economics — it is a policy choice. Every currency sits somewhere on the spectrum between fixed and floating, and where it sits changes how it behaves for everyone who uses it.

Floating rates: the market decides

Under a floating regime, a currency's price is set purely by supply and demand on the foreign exchange market. The US dollar, euro, British pound, Japanese yen and most other major currencies float. If investors want more euros, the euro rises; if a country's economy weakens, its currency tends to fall, which automatically makes its exports cheaper and imports dearer — a built-in shock absorber for the economy.

The cost of floating is uncertainty. Businesses trading abroad face constantly changing prices, travellers watch their holiday money gain or lose value, and governments give up control over one of the most visible prices in the economy.

Fixed rates: the government decides — and must defend the decision

A fixed, or pegged, exchange rate works the opposite way. The government or central bank declares that its currency is worth a set amount of another currency — the anchor — and commits to keeping it there. The Saudi riyal has been pegged at 3.75 per US dollar since 1986; the UAE dirham at about 3.6725; the Hong Kong dollar trades in a tight band around 7.80.

A peg is not maintained by declaration alone. The central bank must stand ready to buy its own currency with foreign reserves whenever the market pushes the rate below the peg, and to sell its currency whenever the market pushes it above. That requires large reserves of the anchor currency and a willingness to use them. In effect, the country imports the anchor country's monetary policy: if the US Federal Reserve raises interest rates, a dollar-pegged central bank generally must follow, whether or not its own economy needs it.

Why would a country choose a peg?

  • Stability for trade. If most of your trade and debt is in dollars, a stable dollar rate removes exchange risk from everyday business.
  • Credibility against inflation. A country with a history of high inflation can borrow the anchor's credibility: tying the currency to a stable one signals that money printing is off the table.
  • Simplicity for citizens. Prices, savings and salaries are insulated from currency swings; a remittance sent home is worth the same this month as last.

The Gulf oil exporters illustrate the logic: their main export is priced in dollars, their imports come from everywhere, and deep reserves make the peg easy to defend.

When pegs break

A peg survives only as long as the central bank has the reserves — and the political will — to defend it. If a country's economy weakens, reserves drain, and traders begin to suspect the peg cannot hold, speculation itself accelerates the crisis: everyone tries to exit the currency at the guaranteed price before it changes. Britain's forced exit from the European Exchange Rate Mechanism in 1992, and the collapses that swept Asia in 1997, both followed this script. When a peg breaks, the currency typically falls sharply and suddenly — the opposite of the stability the peg was built to provide.

The middle ground: bands, crawls and managed floats

Most real-world systems live between the extremes:

  • Banded pegs let the rate move within a declared range around a central value — Hong Kong's 7.75–7.85 band against the dollar is the classic example.
  • Crawling pegs adjust the fixed rate gradually on a schedule, often to offset higher domestic inflation.
  • Managed floats let the market set the rate day to day, but the central bank intervenes to smooth sharp moves. India and Pakistan both operate versions of this model — the rate floats, but not entirely alone.

What the regime means for your money

For everyday conversions, the practical differences are these. Converting between two floating currencies means accepting that the rate moves — timing and provider margins both matter, as our article on the best time to exchange explains. Converting into a dollar-pegged currency means the dollar rate is effectively known in advance; what varies is only your provider's margin. And converting into a currency whose peg looks strained deserves extra care: hold as little of it as you need, because the risk is not gradual drift but a sudden step down.

In short: floating currencies absorb shocks by moving; fixed currencies resist shocks by spending reserves. Neither is "better" — they are different answers to the question of who should bear the uncertainty, the market or the state.

How to Read Currency Pairs: EUR/USD and Quotes Explained

EUR/USD 1.0850. GBP/USD 1.2713. USD/PKR, USD/JPY, EUR/GBP — currency quotes look like code, but they follow one simple pattern. Once you can read a pair, every converter, news headline and bank quote becomes instantly clearer, including knowing which of the two numbers on a dealer's board applies to you.

Why currencies are quoted in pairs

A currency has no price on its own — it only has a price in terms of another currency. A euro is worth some number of dollars, some number of pounds, some number of yen. So every exchange rate is a ratio between two currencies, written as a pair: the two currency codes separated by a slash. The codes themselves are the standard three-letter ISO codes used worldwide: USD for the US dollar, EUR for the euro, GBP for the British pound, JPY for the Japanese yen, PKR for the Pakistani rupee, and so on.

Base currency and quote currency

In any pair, the first currency is the base currency and the second is the quote currency. The number that follows the pair tells you how much of the quote currency one unit of the base currency buys:

EUR/USD = 1.0850 → 1 euro buys 1.0850 US dollars USD/JPY = 155.40 → 1 US dollar buys 155.40 Japanese yen USD/PKR = 278.50 → 1 US dollar buys 278.50 Pakistani rupees

The base currency is always the one being priced — think of it as the item on the shelf — and the quote currency is the money you pay for it. To convert an amount of the base currency, multiply by the rate. To convert the other way, divide. Our converter handles the arithmetic in both directions, but knowing which way round a quote runs stops the classic mistake of multiplying when you should have divided.

Which currency goes first? The unwritten hierarchy

By market convention, pairs are written in a standard order rather than whichever way someone feels like. The euro always comes before the dollar (EUR/USD, never USD/EUR in professional quotes), the pound comes before the dollar (GBP/USD), and the dollar comes before the yen, franc and most other currencies (USD/JPY, USD/CHF, USD/PKR). Roughly, the convention ranks the euro first, then the pound, then the Australian and New Zealand dollars, then the US dollar, followed by the rest. You do not need to memorise the full order — just remember that the pair's direction is fixed by convention, so if you see a rate that looks like the upside-down version of what you expected, you are probably looking at the pair written the other way round.

Every quote has two prices: bid and ask

Professional quotes show two numbers, such as:

EUR/USD 1.0850 / 1.0852 bid / ask

The bid is the price at which the dealer buys the base currency from you; the ask (or offer) is the price at which the dealer sells it to you. The ask is always slightly higher — that gap, the spread, is the dealer's margin. The midpoint between them is the mid-market rate, the benchmark this site displays. Our guide to mid-market, buy and sell rates walks through exactly how these three prices relate and how wide a spread is fair.

Pips: the unit of movement

Traders measure rate movements in pips — for most pairs, a pip is the fourth decimal place, 0.0001. If EUR/USD moves from 1.0850 to 1.0870, it has risen 20 pips. For pairs involving the yen, where rates are quoted to two decimals, a pip is 0.01. You will never need pips to exchange holiday money, but news reports that "the pound gained 40 pips" simply mean the rate moved 0.0040 — pips are just a convenient shorthand for small movements.

Majors, minors and exotics

  • Major pairs combine the US dollar with another heavily traded currency: EUR/USD (the most traded pair in the world), USD/JPY, GBP/USD, USD/CHF, AUD/USD, USD/CAD and NZD/USD. They have the tightest spreads and the smoothest prices.
  • Minor pairs (or crosses) pair two major currencies without the dollar — EUR/GBP, EUR/JPY, GBP/JPY. Their rates are derived through the dollar behind the scenes, as explained in How Currency Conversion Works.
  • Exotic pairs combine a major currency with one from a smaller economy — USD/PKR, USD/TRY, EUR/PLN. They trade less heavily, so spreads are wider and rates can jump more sharply.

Reading a quote like a dealer

Put it together with a real-world example. A bureau displays "USD/PKR: buy 277.10, sell 280.30". The pair tells you the price is rupees per dollar. "Buy" and "sell" are from the bureau's point of view: it buys your dollars at 277.10 rupees each and sells dollars to you at 280.30. The mid-market rate sits between them, around 278.7 — check it on this site and you instantly know the bureau's spread is roughly half a percent each way, which is a fair retail deal. That ten-second reading skill — pair, direction, bid, ask, midpoint — is genuinely all there is to decoding any currency quote you will ever meet.

Quick recap: first currency = base (what is priced), second = quote (what you pay), the number = how much quote currency buys one unit of base. Bid is what dealers pay you, ask is what they charge you, and mid-market is the honest midpoint in between.
