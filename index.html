How Currency Conversion Works

Every currency conversion — whether you are changing holiday money, paying an overseas invoice or sending funds to family abroad — follows the same basic mechanics. Understanding them takes five minutes and can save you real money for the rest of your life.

It starts with an exchange rate

An exchange rate is simply a price: how much of one currency you must give up to receive one unit of another. When you read that 1 US dollar equals 0.92 euros, the dollar is the base currency and the euro is the quote currency. Converting is then plain arithmetic — multiply your amount by the rate.

250 USD × 0.92 = 230 EUR

That is the whole calculation. Everything else in the world of currency conversion — the fees, the margins, the different prices at different providers — is about which rate gets used in that multiplication, and what gets taken off the top before the money reaches you.

Where exchange rates come from

Currencies trade on the global foreign exchange market, the largest financial market in the world, where banks, governments, funds and large companies buy and sell currencies around the clock. The price moves constantly in response to interest rates, inflation, trade figures, political events and plain supply and demand. There is no single official price set by a committee; there is a market price that emerges from millions of trades.

The rate shown on CurrencyConverterHub is the mid-market rate: the midpoint between the highest price buyers are offering and the lowest price sellers are accepting at that moment. It is the cleanest reference point available, because no single provider's profit margin is baked into it.

Cross rates: converting between two non-dollar currencies

Most published rates are quoted against the US dollar, because the dollar sits on one side of the majority of the world's currency trades. To convert between two other currencies — say, British pounds to Pakistani rupees — the market works through the dollar in two steps: pounds to dollars, then dollars to rupees. This is called a cross rate, and it is exactly what our converter does behind the scenes:

GBP → PKR rate = (USD per GBP) × (PKR per USD)

The result is mathematically identical to trading the pair directly, and it means a converter with dollar-based data can still price any pair of currencies accurately.

Why the amount you receive is less than the calculator says

If the mid-market calculation says your 250 dollars should become 230 euros, a bank might hand you 224. Three things eat the difference:

  • The spread. Providers buy currency at one price and sell it at a slightly worse one. The gap between their buy and sell prices is profit, and it is charged invisibly inside the rate itself.
  • Fixed fees. Many providers add a visible charge per transfer — a few dollars for an online service, considerably more for an international wire at a traditional bank.
  • Correspondent charges. International bank transfers pass through intermediary banks, and each one may deduct a fee before the money arrives.

None of this is necessarily unfair — moving money across borders costs money to do safely. The problem is only that the biggest cost, the spread, is usually hidden. Comparing any quote against the mid-market rate on this page makes it visible in seconds.

Fixed, floating and pegged currencies

Most major currencies float freely: their price is set entirely by the market. Some governments instead peg their currency to another — the Hong Kong dollar tracks the US dollar within a tight band, for example — and a few fix the rate by law. Pegged currencies move in step with their anchor, which is why their rates against the dollar barely change from day to day while their rates against everything else move exactly as the anchor does.

A practical routine for converting money

  • Check the mid-market rate here first, so you know the fair value of your money.
  • Get quotes from two or three providers and compare the final amount you would receive — not the fee alone, and not the rate alone.
  • Watch out for "zero commission" offers; the cost is almost always inside the rate instead.
  • For large amounts, remember rates move. A 1% swing on a house deposit is serious money, so convert close to the day you need the funds.

The one-sentence version: conversion is just multiplication — your amount times an exchange rate — and the only real question is how far the rate you are offered sits from the mid-market rate shown on this site.

Understanding Exchange Rates: Mid-Market, Buy and Sell Rates

Ask three different providers what a euro costs and you will get three different answers — all of them honest, all of them different. That is because a currency never has one price. It has at least three, and knowing which is which is the key to never overpaying for an exchange again.

The buy rate and the sell rate

Every currency dealer quotes two prices at the same time. The buy rate (also called the bid) is the price at which the dealer will buy a currency from you. The sell rate (the ask or offer) is the price at which the dealer will sell that currency to you. The sell rate is always less favourable to you than the buy rate — the dealer sells dear and buys cheap, exactly like any other merchant.

Suppose a bureau quotes the euro at 0.90 to buy and 0.96 to sell, per US dollar's worth of trade. Hand over 100 euros and you receive about 90 dollars. Immediately buy those euros back and you would need about 96 dollars. Nothing about the market moved; the six-dollar difference is the dealer's margin, called the spread.

The mid-market rate sits exactly in between

Take the average of the buy and sell prices and you get the mid-market rate — 0.93 in the example above. This is the rate quoted by news services, search engines and reference converters like this one. No customer actually trades at the mid-market rate; it exists as a neutral benchmark, the "true" market price before any individual provider adds its costs.

Think of the mid-market rate as the wholesale price of currency, and the buy and sell rates as retail prices. The further a retail quote sits from wholesale, the more the exchange is costing you.

How wide is a normal spread?

It depends heavily on where you exchange and what you exchange:

  • Major pairs, online specialists (USD/EUR, GBP/USD): often well under 1% from mid-market.
  • Traditional banks: commonly 1% to 3%, sometimes more for less common currencies.
  • Airport and hotel bureaus: frequently 5% to 10% or worse — the most expensive place to change money.
  • Less traded currencies: wider spreads everywhere, because dealers hold them less and price in more risk.

On a small holiday exchange, a 3% spread costs a few dollars. On a 20,000-dollar transfer, it costs 600 — which is why the spread matters far more than any advertised fee.

"No commission" does not mean free

A bureau can truthfully advertise zero commission while quoting a sell rate 8% away from mid-market. The commission has not disappeared; it has moved into the rate, where most customers never think to look. The only reliable test of any offer is to compare the final amount you receive against the mid-market calculation for the same amount, on the same day.

Which rate should you use for planning?

Use the mid-market rate — the one this site shows — as your reference for budgeting, invoicing and comparing quotes. Then expect the amount you actually receive to be a little lower, and treat anything within about 1% of mid-market on a major currency pair as a fair retail deal. If a quote is 4% or more away, shop around: on any significant sum, a few minutes of comparison is the best-paid work you will do that day.

Best Time to Exchange Currency: Does Timing Really Matter?

Everyone who has watched an exchange rate for a few days has had the same thought: if I had exchanged last Tuesday, I would have got more. So is there a best time to exchange currency — and can an ordinary person actually use it? The honest answer is "sometimes, a little" — and the details matter more than the timing.

First, the uncomfortable truth about predicting rates

Exchange rates are set by a market where professional traders, central banks and algorithms move trillions of dollars every day. If the future direction of a currency were reliably predictable, those professionals would already have traded on it — and their trading would move the price until the opportunity vanished. This is why decades of research keep reaching the same conclusion: short-term currency movements are close to random, and no app, chart pattern or tipster can tell you next week's rate with dependable accuracy.

That does not make timing pointless. It means timing should be treated as risk management, not as a way to outsmart the market. You cannot reliably buy at the bottom, but you can avoid exchanging at obviously bad moments, and you can structure a large conversion so that one unlucky day does not decide the whole outcome.

What actually moves an exchange rate

Understanding the forces behind a rate helps you recognise the moments when a move is likely to be sharp — even if you cannot know its direction in advance:

  • Interest rate decisions. When a central bank raises rates, holding that currency pays more, and demand for it usually rises. Rate announcements are scheduled, so the risky moments are known in advance.
  • Inflation and jobs data. Monthly figures that surprise the market can move a major currency by half a percent or more within minutes.
  • Politics and crises. Elections, trade disputes and conflicts push money toward "safe haven" currencies such as the US dollar, Swiss franc and Japanese yen.
  • Long-term trends. Over months and years, rates follow economic fundamentals — growth, inflation, government debt and trade balances. Trends are real, but they reverse without asking permission.

The weekly rhythm: days and hours

Currency markets open on Monday morning in Asia and close on Friday evening in New York. Within that week, a few patterns are worth knowing:

  • Weekends are frozen. Rates barely move from Friday's close to Monday's open, so the rate you see on Saturday is really Friday's rate. Any weekend news lands all at once on Monday, which is why Monday opens can jump.
  • Midweek is the most liquid. Tuesday to Thursday, when all major financial centres are active, spreads are tightest and prices are smoothest. That makes midweek the calmest time to exchange — not because rates are better, but because they are less jumpy.
  • Avoid exchanging during major announcements if you can. In the minutes around a central bank decision, spreads widen dramatically at many providers, so even a "good" rate can come with a bad spread.

When timing genuinely matters: large amounts

Do the arithmetic and the picture becomes clear. On a 300-dollar holiday exchange, a 1% rate movement is worth 3 dollars — far less than the difference between a good provider and a bad one, which can easily be 5% or more. Chasing timing on small amounts is effort spent in the wrong place.

On a 50,000-dollar conversion — a house deposit, a business payment, a year of overseas tuition — that same 1% is 500 dollars, and a 3% swing across a month is 1,500. At that scale, timing deserves real attention, and these are the strategies professionals actually use:

  • Split the conversion. Exchange in three or four tranches over several weeks. You will get the average rate rather than one day's rate, which removes the risk of picking the single worst day.
  • Set a target rate and a deadline. Decide in advance: "I will exchange if the rate reaches X, and in any case by date Y." This replaces emotion with a plan and prevents the endless waiting that ends in a forced exchange at whatever rate the deadline brings.
  • Use rate alerts. Most converter and transfer apps let you set an alert for a rate level. It costs nothing and means you only pay attention when it matters.
  • Start early. The worst exchanges happen under time pressure. If you know a payment is due in three months, you have options; if it is due on Friday, you have none.

The factor that beats timing every time

Compare two travellers exchanging 1,000 dollars. One waits a week for a rate that improves by 0.4% and gains 4 dollars — then exchanges at an airport bureau with an 8% margin and loses 80. The other exchanges immediately through a provider priced within 0.5% of the mid-market rate. Timing was never the main event; the provider's margin was. Our guide to mid-market, buy and sell rates shows how to measure that margin in seconds, and it is worth more than any timing strategy for typical amounts.

The bottom line: you cannot predict exchange rates, and for everyday amounts you do not need to. Exchange midweek when markets are calm, avoid deadline pressure, split very large conversions into tranches — and spend the rest of your energy on the spread, where the real money is lost and saved.

Currency Conversion Fees Explained: How to Pay Less When You Travel

Ask a traveller what their currency exchange cost and they will usually name the fee they saw — a few dollars at the bureau, maybe a charge on a card statement. The uncomfortable truth is that the visible fee is often the smallest part. This article walks through every way a conversion takes its cut, and how to keep each one as small as possible.

Cost number one: the exchange rate margin

The largest cost in most conversions never appears as a fee at all. Providers buy currency at the mid-market rate — the benchmark shown on this site — and sell it to you at a worse one. The difference is the margin, or spread, and it is charged invisibly inside the rate itself.

Mid-market rate: 1 USD = 0.92 EUR Bureau's rate: 1 USD = 0.87 EUR Hidden cost on 500 USD: about 25 EUR — with a sign saying "0% commission"

Margins vary enormously. Online specialists on major currency pairs often work within 0.5% to 1% of mid-market. High-street banks commonly take 2% to 4%. Airport and hotel bureaus can take 8% or more. Because the margin hides in the rate, the only way to compare providers honestly is to compare the final amount you receive for the same amount sent — never the advertised fee, and never the word "free".

Cost number two: explicit fees

On top of the margin, many providers charge visible fees:

  • Flat transfer fees. A fixed charge per transaction — a few dollars from an online service, but potentially 25 to 45 dollars for an international wire at a traditional bank.
  • Percentage fees. Some services charge a percentage of the amount sent, sometimes combined with a smaller margin in the rate. A low margin plus a percentage fee can still beat a "no fee" offer with a fat margin — always compare totals.
  • Intermediary bank charges. International wires pass through correspondent banks, and each may deduct a fee in transit, so the recipient gets less than the sender sent even when the sending bank charged nothing extra.
  • Delivery and cash pick-up fees. Sending to cash pick-up or a mobile wallet sometimes costs more than sending to a bank account.

Cost number three: dynamic currency conversion (DCC)

You are abroad, you pay by card, and the terminal asks: pay in your home currency or the local one? Choosing your home currency feels safer — you can see the amount in familiar money — but it is one of the most expensive buttons in travel. This is dynamic currency conversion: the merchant's payment company converts the bill at a rate it chooses, typically 3% to 8% worse than mid-market, and shares the profit with the merchant.

The rule is simple: always choose to pay in the local currency of the country you are in. Your own bank or card network will then do the conversion, almost always at a far better rate. The same rule applies at foreign ATMs that offer to "convert for you" — decline the conversion and let your bank handle it.

Cost number four: card foreign transaction fees

Many bank cards add a foreign transaction fee — commonly 1% to 3% — on every purchase made in another currency, on top of the card network's conversion rate (which is usually close to mid-market). On a two-week trip spending 2,000 dollars, a 3% fee is 60 dollars spent on nothing. Before travelling, check your card's terms: plenty of travel-oriented cards charge no foreign transaction fee at all, and using one is the single easiest saving most travellers can make. The same check applies to ATM withdrawals abroad, where some banks add both a percentage fee and a fixed charge per withdrawal — making a few larger withdrawals cheaper than many small ones.

Cash, cards and apps: a practical pecking order

  • Best for most spending: a card with no foreign transaction fee, always paying in the local currency. Near mid-market rates, no cash risk.
  • Best for cash: withdraw from local bank ATMs after arrival, declining the ATM's own conversion offer. Avoid exchanging cash at airports and hotels except in an emergency.
  • Best for sending money abroad: a specialist transfer service compared on the total received — rate and fee together — against the mid-market benchmark.
  • Worst almost everywhere: airport bureaus, hotel desks, and any offer that leads with "zero commission".

A five-minute pre-trip checklist

  • Check every card you plan to carry for foreign transaction fees; leave the expensive ones at home.
  • Memorise the DCC rule: local currency, every time, on terminals and ATMs alike.
  • Look up the mid-market rate for your destination on this converter before you go, so you can recognise a bad quote on sight.
  • Tell your bank where you are travelling, so legitimate foreign transactions are not blocked.
  • Carry a small amount of local cash for arrival — bought at a reasonable rate, not an airport one — and a backup card stored separately.

None of these steps requires predicting exchange rates or opening new accounts weeks in advance. Conversion costs are mostly a matter of knowing where they hide — the margin in the rate, the conversion button on the terminal, the percentage buried in your card's terms. Now that you know, you will pay a fraction of what the average traveller pays.

USD to PKR: How the Dollar to Pakistani Rupee Rate Is Set

Anyone who sends money to Pakistan, prices imports in dollars, or simply watches the news has seen the headline: "the rupee gained" or "the rupee fell". But who actually decides how many rupees one dollar is worth? There is no single price-setter — there is a market, a central bank watching over it, and a set of forces that push the rate every working day.

A managed float, not a fixed price

The Pakistani rupee operates under what economists call a managed float. The rate is determined by supply and demand for dollars in Pakistan's foreign exchange market — it is not fixed by law at a set number — but the State Bank of Pakistan (SBP), the country's central bank, monitors the market and can intervene when movements become disorderly. In practice this means the rate moves daily with market conditions, while the central bank acts as a referee with deep pockets: it can buy dollars to slow a sharp rise in the rate, or sell dollars from its reserves to slow a sharp fall.

This arrangement replaced earlier, more tightly controlled regimes. The modern framework — formally adopted in 2019 as a market-based exchange rate system — was designed so that the rate adjusts gradually to economic conditions instead of being held still for long periods and then jumping in sudden devaluations, as happened repeatedly in Pakistan's past.

The interbank market: where the headline rate is made

The USD/PKR rate quoted in the news and shown on converters like this one comes from the interbank market, where commercial banks trade foreign currency with each other and with the central bank. Importers buy dollars through their banks to pay foreign suppliers; exporters sell the dollars they earn; the balance of those flows, minute by minute, sets the rate.

The State Bank publishes reference rates based on this market, and banks price their customer transactions around it — adding a margin, exactly as dealers do everywhere (see our guide to buy and sell rates). When you transfer money to a Pakistani bank account through a formal channel, the conversion happens at a rate derived from the interbank market.

Why there is also an "open market" rate

Alongside the interbank market, Pakistan has an open market: licensed exchange companies and currency shops that buy and sell physical cash and handle walk-in remittances. Their rates are close to the interbank rate but rarely identical, because they deal in cash, serve different customers, and adjust for their own supply of notes.

When the two rates drift far apart, it signals stress — typically that dollars are scarce in the formal system or that demand is leaking into informal channels. In calm periods the gap is small; during the currency pressure of 2022–23 it widened dramatically, and narrowing it again became an explicit policy goal. If you are comparing rates, make sure you know which market a quote comes from: an open-market cash rate and an interbank transfer rate are cousins, not twins.

The forces that push the rupee

  • Workers' remittances. Pakistanis working abroad — especially in Saudi Arabia, the UAE, the UK and the US — send home tens of billions of dollars a year. This is one of the largest sources of dollar supply, and strong remittance months directly support the rupee.
  • Exports and imports. Pakistan exports textiles, rice and other goods, earning dollars; it imports oil, machinery and food, spending them. Because imports usually exceed exports, there is structural demand for dollars that remittances and other inflows must cover.
  • Foreign reserves. The State Bank's reserve level is the market's confidence gauge. Rising reserves calm the rate; falling reserves make traders nervous and can feed speculation against the rupee.
  • IMF programmes and loans. Agreements with the International Monetary Fund, plus loans and deposits from friendly countries, bring in dollars and carry conditions — including keeping the exchange rate market-based. News about these programmes routinely moves the rate.
  • Interest rates and inflation. High domestic interest rates make holding rupees more attractive; high inflation does the opposite, eroding the currency's value over time.

What this means when you send money to Pakistan

For someone converting dollars to rupees, the practical lessons are these. First, the rate you are quoted by any provider should be compared against the interbank-based mid-market rate — the one shown by this converter — because that is the benchmark the whole formal system prices from. Second, expect the rate to move: a managed float still floats, and a difference of a rupee or two per dollar between Monday and Friday is normal market weather, not a sign anyone is cheating you. Third, the gap between providers is usually worth more than the gap between days — on a 500-dollar remittance, a provider 1% closer to mid-market puts roughly five dollars' more rupees in the recipient's hands, every single time.

In one sentence: the dollar–rupee rate is set by supply and demand in the interbank market — driven by remittances, trade and reserves — with the State Bank of Pakistan smoothing the extremes rather than fixing the price.

Fixed vs Floating Exchange Rates: What a Currency Peg Means

Check the US dollar against the Saudi riyal today, next month, or five years from now, and you will find essentially the same number. Check it against the Japanese yen and it will have moved by the time you finish this article. The difference is not an accident of economics — it is a policy choice. Every currency sits somewhere on the spectrum between fixed and floating, and where it sits changes how it behaves for everyone who uses it.

Floating rates: the market decides

Under a floating regime, a currency's price is set purely by supply and demand on the foreign exchange market. The US dollar, euro, British pound, Japanese yen and most other major currencies float. If investors want more euros, the euro rises; if a country's economy weakens, its currency tends to fall, which automatically makes its exports cheaper and imports dearer — a built-in shock absorber for the economy.

The cost of floating is uncertainty. Businesses trading abroad face constantly changing prices, travellers watch their holiday money gain or lose value, and governments give up control over one of the most visible prices in the economy.

Fixed rates: the government decides — and must defend the decision

A fixed, or pegged, exchange rate works the opposite way. The government or central bank declares that its currency is worth a set amount of another currency — the anchor — and commits to keeping it there. The Saudi riyal has been pegged at 3.75 per US dollar since 1986; the UAE dirham at about 3.6725; the Hong Kong dollar trades in a tight band around 7.80.

A peg is not maintained by declaration alone. The central bank must stand ready to buy its own currency with foreign reserves whenever the market pushes the rate below the peg, and to sell its currency whenever the market pushes it above. That requires large reserves of the anchor currency and a willingness to use them. In effect, the country imports the anchor country's monetary policy: if the US Federal Reserve raises interest rates, a dollar-pegged central bank generally must follow, whether or not its own economy needs it.

Why would a country choose a peg?

  • Stability for trade. If most of your trade and debt is in dollars, a stable dollar rate removes exchange risk from everyday business.
  • Credibility against inflation. A country with a history of high inflation can borrow the anchor's credibility: tying the currency to a stable one signals that money printing is off the table.
  • Simplicity for citizens. Prices, savings and salaries are insulated from currency swings; a remittance sent home is worth the same this month as last.

The Gulf oil exporters illustrate the logic: their main export is priced in dollars, their imports come from everywhere, and deep reserves make the peg easy to defend.

When pegs break

A peg survives only as long as the central bank has the reserves — and the political will — to defend it. If a country's economy weakens, reserves drain, and traders begin to suspect the peg cannot hold, speculation itself accelerates the crisis: everyone tries to exit the currency at the guaranteed price before it changes. Britain's forced exit from the European Exchange Rate Mechanism in 1992, and the collapses that swept Asia in 1997, both followed this script. When a peg breaks, the currency typically falls sharply and suddenly — the opposite of the stability the peg was built to provide.

The middle ground: bands, crawls and managed floats

Most real-world systems live between the extremes:

  • Banded pegs let the rate move within a declared range around a central value — Hong Kong's 7.75–7.85 band against the dollar is the classic example.
  • Crawling pegs adjust the fixed rate gradually on a schedule, often to offset higher domestic inflation.
  • Managed floats let the market set the rate day to day, but the central bank intervenes to smooth sharp moves. India and Pakistan both operate versions of this model — the rate floats, but not entirely alone.

What the regime means for your money

For everyday conversions, the practical differences are these. Converting between two floating currencies means accepting that the rate moves — timing and provider margins both matter, as our article on the best time to exchange explains. Converting into a dollar-pegged currency means the dollar rate is effectively known in advance; what varies is only your provider's margin. And converting into a currency whose peg looks strained deserves extra care: hold as little of it as you need, because the risk is not gradual drift but a sudden step down.

In short: floating currencies absorb shocks by moving; fixed currencies resist shocks by spending reserves. Neither is "better" — they are different answers to the question of who should bear the uncertainty, the market or the state.

How to Read Currency Pairs: EUR/USD and Quotes Explained

EUR/USD 1.0850. GBP/USD 1.2713. USD/PKR, USD/JPY, EUR/GBP — currency quotes look like code, but they follow one simple pattern. Once you can read a pair, every converter, news headline and bank quote becomes instantly clearer, including knowing which of the two numbers on a dealer's board applies to you.

Why currencies are quoted in pairs

A currency has no price on its own — it only has a price in terms of another currency. A euro is worth some number of dollars, some number of pounds, some number of yen. So every exchange rate is a ratio between two currencies, written as a pair: the two currency codes separated by a slash. The codes themselves are the standard three-letter ISO codes used worldwide: USD for the US dollar, EUR for the euro, GBP for the British pound, JPY for the Japanese yen, PKR for the Pakistani rupee, and so on.

Base currency and quote currency

In any pair, the first currency is the base currency and the second is the quote currency. The number that follows the pair tells you how much of the quote currency one unit of the base currency buys:

EUR/USD = 1.0850 → 1 euro buys 1.0850 US dollars USD/JPY = 155.40 → 1 US dollar buys 155.40 Japanese yen USD/PKR = 278.50 → 1 US dollar buys 278.50 Pakistani rupees

The base currency is always the one being priced — think of it as the item on the shelf — and the quote currency is the money you pay for it. To convert an amount of the base currency, multiply by the rate. To convert the other way, divide. Our converter handles the arithmetic in both directions, but knowing which way round a quote runs stops the classic mistake of multiplying when you should have divided.

Which currency goes first? The unwritten hierarchy

By market convention, pairs are written in a standard order rather than whichever way someone feels like. The euro always comes before the dollar (EUR/USD, never USD/EUR in professional quotes), the pound comes before the dollar (GBP/USD), and the dollar comes before the yen, franc and most other currencies (USD/JPY, USD/CHF, USD/PKR). Roughly, the convention ranks the euro first, then the pound, then the Australian and New Zealand dollars, then the US dollar, followed by the rest. You do not need to memorise the full order — just remember that the pair's direction is fixed by convention, so if you see a rate that looks like the upside-down version of what you expected, you are probably looking at the pair written the other way round.

Every quote has two prices: bid and ask

Professional quotes show two numbers, such as:

EUR/USD 1.0850 / 1.0852 bid / ask

The bid is the price at which the dealer buys the base currency from you; the ask (or offer) is the price at which the dealer sells it to you. The ask is always slightly higher — that gap, the spread, is the dealer's margin. The midpoint between them is the mid-market rate, the benchmark this site displays. Our guide to mid-market, buy and sell rates walks through exactly how these three prices relate and how wide a spread is fair.

Pips: the unit of movement

Traders measure rate movements in pips — for most pairs, a pip is the fourth decimal place, 0.0001. If EUR/USD moves from 1.0850 to 1.0870, it has risen 20 pips. For pairs involving the yen, where rates are quoted to two decimals, a pip is 0.01. You will never need pips to exchange holiday money, but news reports that "the pound gained 40 pips" simply mean the rate moved 0.0040 — pips are just a convenient shorthand for small movements.

Majors, minors and exotics

  • Major pairs combine the US dollar with another heavily traded currency: EUR/USD (the most traded pair in the world), USD/JPY, GBP/USD, USD/CHF, AUD/USD, USD/CAD and NZD/USD. They have the tightest spreads and the smoothest prices.
  • Minor pairs (or crosses) pair two major currencies without the dollar — EUR/GBP, EUR/JPY, GBP/JPY. Their rates are derived through the dollar behind the scenes, as explained in How Currency Conversion Works.
  • Exotic pairs combine a major currency with one from a smaller economy — USD/PKR, USD/TRY, EUR/PLN. They trade less heavily, so spreads are wider and rates can jump more sharply.

Reading a quote like a dealer

Put it together with a real-world example. A bureau displays "USD/PKR: buy 277.10, sell 280.30". The pair tells you the price is rupees per dollar. "Buy" and "sell" are from the bureau's point of view: it buys your dollars at 277.10 rupees each and sells dollars to you at 280.30. The mid-market rate sits between them, around 278.7 — check it on this site and you instantly know the bureau's spread is roughly half a percent each way, which is a fair retail deal. That ten-second reading skill — pair, direction, bid, ask, midpoint — is genuinely all there is to decoding any currency quote you will ever meet.

Quick recap: first currency = base (what is priced), second = quote (what you pay), the number = how much quote currency buys one unit of base. Bid is what dealers pay you, ask is what they charge you, and mid-market is the honest midpoint in between.

The Mid-Market Rate Explained: The Rate You See but Rarely Get

Search for any currency conversion and the first number you see is the mid-market rate. It is the rate quoted on the news, shown by reference converters like this one, and used by banks when they trade with each other. Yet when you actually exchange money, you will almost never receive it. Understanding why — and what to do about it — is the single most useful thing you can learn about currency.

What "mid-market" actually means

On the global currency market, every currency has two prices at any moment: the highest price buyers are currently willing to pay, and the lowest price sellers are currently willing to accept. The mid-market rate is exactly what its name suggests — the midpoint between those two prices. It is not set by any government or committee. It emerges continuously from millions of trades between banks, funds and large companies, and it shifts second by second while markets are open.

Because the midpoint contains no profit margin for anyone, economists and journalists treat it as the fairest possible reference point: the "true" market price of a currency before any individual business adds its costs. When this site shows you a rate, it is showing you that midpoint, fetched from live market data — the same benchmark our guide to mid-market, buy and sell rates explains in more depth.

Why you rarely get it

Retail customers do not trade on the global market directly. You trade with a bank, a bureau, a card network or a transfer service, and each of those businesses stands between you and the market. Like any middleman, they buy at one price and sell at another, and the difference — the spread or margin — is how they pay for their branches, apps, staff, licences and risk, and how they make a profit.

So the rate you are offered is the mid-market rate with a slice taken off. On a competitive online service exchanging a major currency pair, that slice might be well under one percent. At a traditional bank it is commonly one to four percent. At an airport bureau it can be far more. The mid-market rate itself has not changed; what changes is how much of it reaches you.

A worked example

Suppose the mid-market rate says one unit of your currency buys exactly one unit of another — a clean one-to-one rate, chosen here just to make the arithmetic obvious. A provider quoting you 0.97 is taking a 3% margin. On a small holiday exchange of a few hundred, that costs you the price of a modest meal. On a transfer of ten thousand, the same 3% costs three hundred — far more than any visible fee the provider might also charge. This is why the margin, invisible as it is, usually matters more than the fee printed on the receipt, a point we return to in Currency Conversion Fees Explained.

The mid-market rate as your measuring stick

You cannot force a provider to give you the mid-market rate, but you can use it to grade every offer in seconds. The routine is simple:

  • Check the mid-market rate for your pair on the currency converter and work out what your amount should become at that rate.
  • Ask the provider for the final amount you would receive — not just their rate, and not just their fee.
  • Divide what they offer by what the mid-market calculation says. The shortfall is the total cost of the exchange, expressed honestly as a percentage.

A shortfall under one percent on a major currency pair is a good retail deal. Two to three percent is typical of a high-street bank. Anything beyond that deserves a second quote from a competitor before you commit — and on a large amount, getting that second quote is some of the best-paid minutes of your life.

Is the mid-market rate ever actually available?

Occasionally, promotions from newer transfer services advertise rates at or extremely close to mid-market, funded by a visible fee instead — a pricing model that is often fairer because the cost is transparent. Large businesses negotiating directly with banks can also get close. But for ordinary retail exchanges, treat the mid-market rate as what it is: a benchmark, not a promise. Its job is to tell you the truth about the market so that every real offer can be judged against it.

In one sentence: the mid-market rate is the market's own midpoint price with nobody's profit inside it — you will rarely trade at it, but you should never trade without knowing it.

Why Banks Give You a Worse Exchange Rate Than Google

It is a moment almost everyone has had: you search a conversion, see a clean rate, walk into your bank — and get offered noticeably less. It feels like a trick. In fact, the two numbers are answers to two different questions, and once you see which is which, bank pricing stops looking mysterious and starts looking like what it is: a retail price for a wholesale product.

Google shows the market rate, not a selling price

When a search engine or a reference converter like this one displays a rate, it is displaying the mid-market rate — the midpoint of prices on the global currency market, where banks trade with each other in enormous volumes. Nobody is offering to sell you currency at that number. It is a measurement, like the wholesale price of coffee beans: accurate, useful, and not what any shop will charge you for a cup.

Your bank, by contrast, is quoting a retail price: an actual offer to complete your exchange, right now, for your amount, with all of its costs and profit included. Comparing the two is fair — indeed, comparing them is exactly how you should judge the bank's price — but it should not surprise you that they differ.

Where the bank's slice goes

A bank's exchange rate has to cover more than the currency itself:

  • Real operating costs. Branches, staff, compliance with money-transfer regulations, fraud checks and the systems that move money between countries all cost money, and currency services help pay for them.
  • Risk. Rates move between the moment a bank quotes you and the moment it settles the trade on the market. A margin is partly insurance against that movement, especially for cash and less common currencies the bank may hold for days.
  • Profit on a captive customer. This is the uncomfortable part. Most customers exchange money rarely, compare prices never, and value the convenience of using the bank they already have. Banks price accordingly — not at the level competition would force, but at the level habit allows.

Layered on top of the margin, many banks add visible charges as well: a flat fee for an international transfer, sometimes further deductions by intermediary banks along the route. Our breakdown of currency conversion fees shows how these costs stack up in practice.

How big is the gap, honestly?

For major currency pairs, a traditional bank's rate commonly sits one to four percent away from the mid-market rate, before any fixed fee. Specialist online transfer services, built with lower overheads and competing on price, often work within half a percent to one percent. On a small amount the difference is pocket change. On a transfer of several thousand — a deposit, tuition, a payment to family — the difference between a bank at three percent and a specialist at half a percent is real money that buys real things in the destination country.

When the bank is still the right choice

None of this makes banks villains or specialists automatically better. A bank transfer can make sense when the amount is small and convenience dominates, when you need a service a specialist cannot offer, or when you value having the whole transaction inside an institution you already trust with your salary. Some banks also price certain currency corridors competitively. The mistake is not using a bank; the mistake is using one without knowing the price. Checking takes thirty seconds: get the bank's final received amount, check the mid-market figure on the converter, and see the percentage for yourself.

The question that cuts through everything

Providers advertise in whatever unit makes them look cheapest — a low fee here, "zero commission" there, a rate quoted to four decimal places somewhere else. There is one question that collapses all of it into a single comparable number: "If I hand you this much, how much will arrive in the other currency?" Ask it of the bank and of one or two competitors, compare the answers against the mid-market benchmark, and the best deal identifies itself — no expertise required.

The bottom line: Google quotes the market; your bank quotes a retail price with its costs and margin inside. The gap is not an error — but its size is your choice, and measuring it costs you nothing.

The Best Way to Exchange Money When Travelling

Every trip involves the same quiet question: how do I turn my money into their money without losing a slice of the holiday budget on the way? There is no single perfect method — but there is a clear ranking, and the difference between the best and worst options on a typical trip is enough to pay for a very nice dinner.

The ranking, from best to worst

For most travellers, in most destinations, the methods line up like this. First and best for everyday spending: a debit or credit card with no foreign transaction fee, used in the local currency. Second: cash withdrawn from local bank ATMs after you arrive, again in the local currency. Third: currency ordered in advance from a competitive online provider. Fourth: a specialist transfer or travel-money service. And last, by a wide margin: airport bureaus, hotel desks and railway-station kiosks, whose convenience is priced into rates that can sit many percent away from the market.

Notice what the top options share: they convert at rates close to the mid-market rate, because the conversion is done by a card network or a bank at near-wholesale prices. The worst options share the opposite: a human, a rent bill in a prime location, and a rate padded to pay for both.

Cards: your primary spending tool

Paying by card abroad converts at the card network's rate, which is typically within a fraction of a percent of mid-market. The catch lives in your own bank's terms: many cards add a foreign transaction fee of one to three percent on top. Before you travel, check every card you own and take the one without the fee if you have it — this single check is the easiest saving in all of travel money. One warning belongs with every card payment abroad: if the terminal offers to charge you in your home currency, decline. That offer is dynamic currency conversion, and it replaces the network's good rate with a bad one chosen by the merchant's payment company.

Cash: still necessary, best sourced locally

Taxis, markets, tips and small shops still run on cash in much of the world. The cheapest way to get it is usually a local ATM belonging to a proper bank, withdrawing in the local currency and declining any "conversion" the machine offers you — your own bank's rate will almost always beat the ATM operator's. Make fewer, larger withdrawals if your bank charges per withdrawal, keep the cash split between your bag and your room, and avoid the standalone ATMs in tourist areas that add their own fees on top of everything else.

A small amount of local currency for arrival day — the taxi from the airport, the first coffee — is worth arranging in advance even at a mediocre rate, simply to remove stress. Just keep that amount small, and source the rest properly once you are settled.

What about exchanging before you go?

Ordering currency from an online travel-money provider can be competitive, especially for major currencies ordered a few days ahead. High-street banks and post offices sit in the middle of the pack. What you should not do is leave it to the airport: departure-hall bureaus face no competition from you once you are standing in front of them, and their rates show it. Whatever route you choose, check the quote against the mid-market rate on the converter before you commit — a quote more than a couple of percent away on a major currency is a quote to walk away from.

A simple plan that covers almost every trip

  • Carry one no-foreign-fee card as your main spender, and a backup card stored separately.
  • Arrive with a small amount of local cash for the first few hours.
  • Top up cash from bank ATMs in the local currency as needed, declining on-screen conversion offers.
  • Always choose "local currency" on card terminals, every single time.
  • Know the rough mid-market rate for your destination so a bad quote is obvious on sight.

Follow that plan and the currency side of travel becomes nearly free of friction and fees — the way it should be, since the money was supposed to fund the trip, not the exchange industry. The traveller's rule of thumb: convert electronically at network rates whenever you can, use local ATMs for cash, and treat every airport bureau and every "pay in your own currency" prompt as the expensive option it is.

What Affects the Value of the US Dollar?

The dollar is the closest thing the world has to a shared currency: it prices oil, anchors dozens of other currencies, and sits on one side of the great majority of all currency trades. When it moves, conversions everywhere move with it. So what actually pushes the dollar up or down? The answer is a handful of forces that are easier to follow than the headlines suggest.

Interest rates: the strongest everyday force

Money flows toward return. When the US Federal Reserve raises interest rates, holding dollars — in savings, bonds or money-market funds — pays more than it did before, and investors around the world shift funds into dollars to capture that return. That extra demand pushes the dollar's price up. When the Fed cuts rates, or when other central banks raise theirs faster, the advantage narrows and the dollar tends to soften. This is why the financial world's calendar revolves around Fed meetings: a single sentence about future rates can move the dollar against every currency at once, which is one reason exchange rates change every day.

Inflation: the slow erosion

A currency is a claim on the goods and services of its country, and inflation shrinks that claim. If prices in the United States rise faster than prices elsewhere, each dollar buys less over time, and the currency tends to weaken against the currencies of lower-inflation countries to compensate. Inflation also matters indirectly: high inflation usually forces the Fed to keep interest rates high, which supports the dollar in the short term even as it erodes it in the long term. That tug-of-war explains why inflation news can move the dollar in either direction depending on what traders expect the Fed to do next. The relationship is explored in depth in Inflation and Currency Value, Explained Simply.

Growth, jobs and the health of the economy

A strong economy attracts investment, and investment requires buying the local currency. Healthy US growth, low unemployment and strong consumer spending all make dollar assets more attractive and tend to support the currency. Weak data does the opposite. Monthly jobs reports and quarterly growth figures are therefore scheduled earthquakes for the dollar: traders compare each release with expectations, and the surprise — good or bad — is what moves the rate in the minutes that follow.

The safe-haven effect

The dollar has a peculiar second job: it is the asset the world runs to in a crisis. During wars, financial panics and global shocks, investors sell riskier holdings and buy US government bonds, prized for their safety and the ease of trading them. That flight to safety requires buying dollars first, so the dollar often rises precisely when the world economy looks worst — even when the crisis begins in the United States itself. The Swiss franc and Japanese yen share some of this status, but none matches the dollar's depth.

Trade, debt and the twin deficits

Over longer horizons, two structural forces weigh on the discussion. The United States imports more than it exports, which means a constant flow of dollars out into the world — a flow that must be recycled back by foreign investors buying US assets for the rate to hold. And the US government borrows heavily, issuing the very bonds the world treats as its safest asset. Economists have debated for decades whether these deficits must eventually weaken the dollar; so far, global demand for dollar assets has absorbed them. What is certain is that any loss of confidence in that arrangement — in the Fed's independence, in the reliability of US institutions, or in the dollar's role in trade — would show up in the exchange rate first.

What this means for your conversions

You do not need to forecast the dollar to convert money sensibly, and professionals with full-time research teams get its direction wrong regularly. What the forces above give you is understanding rather than prediction: when you see the dollar jump after a Fed announcement or slide on weak jobs data, you know why — and you know that a move driven by one day's news may reverse just as quickly. For practical decisions, the guidance in our article on the best time to exchange currency applies: plan large conversions in stages, and spend your energy on provider margins, which you control, rather than on market direction, which nobody does.

In short: the dollar rises when US interest rates and growth look strong and when the world feels frightened; it falls when inflation bites, rates come down, or confidence in its unique role fades. Everything else is commentary on those themes.

Inflation and Currency Value, Explained Simply

Inflation is usually talked about as a shopping problem — prices going up at the supermarket. But it is also an exchange-rate problem. The same force that makes your money buy less at home tends to make it worth less abroad, and the connection between the two explains some of the biggest currency movements in history.

Start with what inflation really is

Inflation is a fall in the purchasing power of money. If prices in a country rise by five percent in a year, then a banknote that bought a full basket of goods in January buys about ninety-five percent of that basket by December. The note looks identical; what it commands has shrunk. Economists measure this with price indexes that track a standard basket of goods and services over time, but the experience needs no index: everyone has watched a familiar item creep up in price.

Why a shrinking currency falls abroad too

Imagine two countries whose currencies trade at par — one unit each way. In the first country, prices rise ten percent over a year; in the second, they stay flat. A product that cost one unit in both countries now costs 1.10 units in the first and still one unit in the second. Traders and travellers notice immediately: money from the second country buys the product more cheaply. Demand shifts toward the stable currency and away from the inflating one, and the exchange rate adjusts until the advantage disappears — meaning the inflating currency falls by roughly the inflation gap. This idea, called purchasing power parity, is explored further in Purchasing Power: What Your Money Is Really Worth Abroad.

In real markets the adjustment is neither instant nor exact — exchange rates overshoot, undershoot and respond to many other forces. But over years and decades, the pattern is one of the most reliable in economics: persistently high inflation and a persistently weakening currency arrive together.

The central bank's countermove: interest rates

There is a complication, and it explains why inflation headlines sometimes strengthen a currency instead of weakening it. When inflation rises, the country's central bank typically raises interest rates to cool the economy. Higher rates make holding that currency more rewarding — savers and investors earn more — and that extra return can attract foreign money and support the exchange rate in the short term, even as inflation erodes it underneath. Which force wins depends on whether traders believe the central bank is serious. A bank that raises rates decisively can stabilise its currency; a bank that is seen as unwilling — or is prevented by its government from acting — watches the currency slide as investors conclude that erosion will continue. How Central Banks Influence Exchange Rates explains the full toolkit behind these decisions.

When inflation runs away: the extreme cases

At the far end of the scale sits hyperinflation, where prices rise not by percent per year but by percent per day, and the currency's value collapses almost to nothing. History's examples — from post-war Europe to more recent cases in Latin America and Africa — share a pattern: a government financing itself by creating money, until the public loses faith and flees into foreign currency, gold or goods. In such episodes the exchange rate does not drift; it disintegrates, and savings held in the local currency are wiped out. These cases are rare, but they demonstrate the principle at full strength: a currency is ultimately worth what people believe it will buy tomorrow.

What this means for ordinary money decisions

  • Savings. Cash held in a high-inflation currency loses value twice — at home through prices, and abroad through the exchange rate. The interest rate on savings needs to beat inflation, not just look large, to protect you.
  • Remittances. Money sent to a high-inflation country buys less each month it sits unspent in local currency. Recipients often convert or spend sooner rather than later, and senders sometimes time transfers around this reality.
  • Salaries and contracts. Anyone paid in a currency different from the one they spend — including the freelancers in our guide to getting paid in a foreign currency — is exposed to this slow drift as well as to daily rate movements.
  • Travel. A destination with high inflation often has a weakening currency, which can make it cheaper for visitors over time — one reason yesterday's expensive city becomes this year's bargain.

The simple version: inflation is a currency quietly shrinking at home; the exchange rate is that same shrinkage measured against other currencies. High inflation, sustained, almost always means a weaker currency — the only question is how fast.

How to Send Money Abroad Cheaply

Sending money internationally is one of those tasks where the price is almost never the price advertised. A service can trumpet "zero fees" and still cost you more than a competitor charging an honest fee, because the real cost hides in two places at once. This article shows you both, and gives you a comparison method that works for any provider, any country and any amount.

The two-part cost of every transfer

Every international transfer takes its cut in exactly two ways. The first is the fee: the visible charge for making the transfer, quoted as a flat amount or a percentage. The second is the exchange rate margin: the gap between the mid-market rate and the rate your provider actually uses. A provider charging no fee but converting three percent below mid-market costs you thirty units on every thousand sent — more than most fee-charging competitors. A provider charging a small fee but converting at the mid-market rate can be the cheapest option on the table while looking like the only one that charges.

This is why fee advertisements are nearly useless for comparison. The only figure that captures both costs at once is the amount the recipient actually receives. Compare that, and you have compared everything.

The comparison method, step by step

  • Fix the amount you want to send, in your own currency.
  • Check the mid-market rate on the converter and calculate what the recipient would get at that perfect rate. This is your benchmark — nobody will quite reach it, but everyone can be measured against it.
  • Get final received-amount quotes from two or three providers for the same send amount, including any delivery charges for cash pick-up or mobile wallets.
  • Divide each quote by your benchmark. The closest to one hundred percent wins, and the gap tells you exactly what each service costs in total.

The whole exercise takes a few minutes and, on a transfer of a few thousand, routinely saves the equivalent of a day's wages. On regular transfers — monthly support to family, rent on a property abroad, paying a contractor — it is worth repeating the check a few times a year, because providers' pricing drifts and introductory offers expire.

Your main options, honestly assessed

Traditional banks are the default and usually the most expensive route for international personal transfers: an outgoing wire fee, a rate margin of one to four percent, and possible deductions by intermediary banks before the money lands. Their strengths are familiarity and handling very large or complex transfers. Specialist online transfer services compete precisely on this market and are typically far cheaper: small transparent fees, margins often under one percent on major routes, and delivery in minutes to days depending on the corridor. Cash-based services — sending to a pick-up location — serve recipients without bank accounts but usually cost more than account-to-account transfers. Sending through a friend travelling home or informal channels carries risks that no saving justifies: no recourse, no record, and in some corridors, legal problems for both parties.

Speed, limits and the fine print

Cheapest is not the only criterion. Delivery speed varies from minutes to several working days, and the fastest option on a route sometimes costs more. First transfers often trigger identity verification, which can add a day — set up and verify your account before the day the money is urgent. Check the receiving side too: some banks charge an incoming wire fee, and some currencies can only be delivered to certain account types. Finally, for very large transfers, expect questions about the source of funds; that is normal anti-money-laundering practice everywhere, not a sign of trouble.

A note on regular transfers

If you send money on a schedule, small optimisations compound. Some services offer slightly better rates for larger amounts, so one monthly transfer may beat four weekly ones. Rate alerts can help you catch favourable days for non-urgent transfers, though — as our article on the best time to exchange explains — the provider's margin will always matter more than timing. The whole method in one line: ignore the fee, ignore the advertised rate, and compare only what arrives — measured against the mid-market benchmark you checked first.

Dynamic Currency Conversion: The Card Machine Trap to Avoid

You are paying for dinner abroad. The card machine turns to you with a friendly question: pay in the local currency, or in your own? Your own currency feels safer — you can see exactly what you are spending. That feeling of safety is precisely what is being sold to you, and it is one of the most expensive buttons in modern travel. This is dynamic currency conversion, and learning to refuse it takes ten seconds.

What is actually happening at that prompt

Normally, when you pay by card abroad, the charge travels through the card network in the local currency and your own bank converts it at the network's rate — a rate close to the mid-market rate, plus any foreign transaction fee your card carries. With dynamic currency conversion (DCC), a different company steps in: the merchant's payment provider converts the bill on the spot, at a rate it chooses itself, and presents the result in your home currency. That rate is not a market rate. It typically contains a markup of several percent — commonly in the range of three to eight percent, sometimes more — and the profit is shared between the payment provider and the merchant who offered you the "convenience".

Nothing about the prompt tells you this. The screen shows two amounts and lets you pick, without disclosing the markup or the rate used. Regulations in some regions now require parts of this to be disclosed, but the disclosure, where it exists, is small print at the exact moment you are least likely to read it.

Why it catches so many people

DCC is engineered around genuine human instincts. Travellers like certainty about what they are spending. People trust numbers in a currency they understand. And the prompt arrives under mild social pressure — a waiter waiting, a queue forming, a machine beeping. Some terminals make the home-currency option the larger, brighter button; some ask the question in a way that makes declining feel like cancelling the payment entirely. None of this is accidental. The industry knows that a large share of travellers, offered the choice quickly and without information, will pick the familiar number — and pay the hidden percentage for it.

The same trap at the ATM

Foreign ATMs run the identical play. After you enter your withdrawal amount, the machine may offer to "convert at a guaranteed rate" or show you the withdrawal in your home currency "for your convenience". Accepting means the ATM operator converts at its own padded rate; declining — sometimes labelled confusingly as "proceed without conversion" — means your own bank converts at its far better rate. Declining the conversion never cancels the withdrawal. The wording is designed to make you fear that it might.

The one rule that defeats it

Always pay — and always withdraw — in the local currency of the country you are in. If the terminal shows your home currency as an option, choose the other one. If staff offer to "put it through in dollars/pounds for you", politely decline. Your bank's conversion, even with a foreign transaction fee, will beat the DCC rate virtually every time.

There is a narrow exception worth knowing: if your own card carries an unusually punitive foreign transaction fee and the DCC markup happens to be small and disclosed, the maths could theoretically favour DCC. In practice the markups are rarely disclosed clearly enough to make that calculation at a till — which is itself the answer. A price you cannot verify is a price you should not accept.

What to do if it happens anyway

If you realise a payment went through with DCC — the receipt showing your home currency with a conversion note is the tell — you have limited options after the fact, since you technically accepted the offer. It is still worth checking the receipt before leaving the shop: an immediate void and re-charge in local currency is sometimes possible while you are standing there. Mostly, treat it as a cheap lesson rather than an expensive habit: on a single meal the loss is small, but across a two-week trip of hotels, meals and shopping, habitual DCC can quietly add the cost of an extra night's stay to your bill. The good news is that this is the rare financial trap with a perfect, costless defence. You do not need a special card, an app or an account. Why Do Exchange Rates Change Every Day?

Nobody sets the price of the pound, the yen or the rupee each morning. There is no committee, no announcement, no official number — yet the rate you see today is never quite the rate you saw yesterday. This article explains what is actually moving underneath: a continuous global auction, and the news that keeps changing what the bidders are willing to pay.

An auction that never really stops

An exchange rate is a price, and like any price it is set by the balance of buyers and sellers at each moment. Currencies trade on the foreign exchange market, a decentralised network of banks, funds, companies and governments exchanging money around the clock — opening in Asia on Monday morning, passing through Europe, closing in New York on Friday evening. At every instant, someone is buying euros to pay a supplier, selling yen after a holiday in reverse, or moving billions between investments. When buying pressure exceeds selling pressure, the price rises until the two balance again; when sellers dominate, it falls. The movement you see day to day is simply that balance shifting, thousands of times.

Who is doing all this buying and selling?

Only a small share of currency trading is people exchanging holiday money. The great flows are commercial and financial: exporters converting the foreign currency they earned, importers buying currency to pay invoices, multinational companies moving profits home, pension funds adjusting international portfolios, and traders taking positions on where rates will go next. Layered on top are central banks managing reserves. Each participant has its own reasons and timetable, which is why the market's balance — and therefore the rate — never sits still while the world is awake and working.

The scheduled earthquakes: economic data

Much of the daily drama follows a calendar. Governments and central banks publish figures on set dates — inflation, employment, economic growth, retail sales, trade balances — and traders compare each number with what was expected. A surprise moves the rate because it changes expectations about the future: stronger-than-expected inflation suggests interest rates will stay higher for longer, which makes holding that currency more rewarding, which pulls buyers in within seconds. Our article on what affects the value of the US dollar walks through this machinery for the world's most watched currency, and inflation and currency value explains the deepest of these relationships.

Interest rate expectations: the master variable

If one force deserves top billing, it is the expected path of interest rates. Money is mobile, and it prefers currencies where it earns more, safely. A central bank signalling higher rates ahead tends to lift its currency immediately — traders do not wait for the actual rise — while hints of cuts push it down. This is why central bank speeches, meeting minutes and even single adjectives in official statements are parsed like scripture by currency markets, a dynamic covered in How Central Banks Influence Exchange Rates.

Politics, crises and the unpredictable

On top of the calendar comes the unscheduled: elections, trade disputes, wars, natural disasters, banking scares. Uncertainty pushes money toward currencies perceived as safe — above all the US dollar, Swiss franc and Japanese yen — and away from currencies of countries directly exposed to the shock. These moves can be sharp and can reverse just as fast when the news changes. They are also the main reason no forecasting service, however confident, can tell you next month's rate: the events that move it most are the ones nobody has scheduled.

Why rates pause on weekends

When the major markets close on Friday, the auction effectively halts. The rate you see over the weekend is Friday's closing price, held over until trading resumes — which is why Monday mornings sometimes open with a jump, as a weekend's worth of news lands on the market all at once. Our converter's rates, drawn from daily-updated market data, reflect this rhythm too.

The picture to keep: an exchange rate is the live score of a global auction between everyone who needs a currency and everyone who holds it. Data releases and interest-rate expectations move the bidders daily; politics moves them suddenly; and the score only ever rests when the market itself is closed.

Some currencies, of course, are not allowed to move freely at all — see Fixed vs Floating Exchange Rates for the other system.

Purchasing Power: What Your Money Is Really Worth Abroad

Convert a salary into another currency and the arithmetic is perfect — but the answer can still mislead you completely. A sum that sounds modest in one country can fund a comfortable life in another, while a fortune converted at a great rate can evaporate in an expensive city. The missing concept is purchasing power: not what your money exchanges for, but what it actually buys.

Two different questions

An exchange rate answers the question: how many units of foreign currency will my money fetch? Purchasing power answers the more human question: once I have those units, what do they get me? The two diverge because prices are not the same everywhere. A haircut, a rented room, a bus ride or a restaurant meal can cost several times more in one country than in another, even after conversion at the exact market rate. Services in particular — anything produced and consumed locally, by local labour — tend to be priced to local incomes, not to world markets. That is why travellers from wealthy countries often feel rich in lower-income destinations, and why visitors going the other way feel the ground drop from under their budget.

Purchasing power parity: the economist's yardstick

Economists formalise this with purchasing power parity, or PPP: the hypothetical exchange rate at which a standard basket of goods would cost the same in both countries. If the basket costs twice as much in country A as in country B, the PPP rate says A's currency is "worth" half of B's for spending purposes — regardless of what the market rate says. International organisations use PPP adjustments to compare living standards fairly: a salary compared at market rates can look poverty-level, while the same salary at PPP turns out to support a middle-class life, because housing, food and services at home are priced for that salary. The most famous popular version of the idea compares the price of an identical burger across countries to judge whether a currency looks cheap or expensive against the dollar — crude, but it captures the intuition in a single number.

Why the gap between market rates and PPP persists

If a currency buys more abroad than the market rate implies, why doesn't the market correct it? Because most of what purchasing power measures cannot be shipped. You cannot import a cheap haircut, a low-rent apartment or an inexpensive taxi ride from another country; those prices are set by local wages and local rents, and they stay local. Tradable goods — phones, fuel, branded products — do converge toward world prices, which is why electronics cost roughly the same everywhere while services vary enormously. As a country's income level rises, its service prices rise toward world levels too, and its PPP and market rates gradually converge — one reason fast-growing economies often see their currencies strengthen over long periods.

Where purchasing power changes real decisions

  • Relocating or retiring abroad. The relevant calculation is never the exchange rate alone; it is your income against local prices for housing, healthcare, food and transport. A pension that is tight at home can be generous somewhere with lower local costs — the entire logic of retirement migration.
  • Remote work and freelancing. Earning in a strong currency while spending in a lower-cost country is a purchasing-power arbitrage, and it is the economic engine behind digital-nomad hubs. The flip side belongs to our freelancers' guide: being paid in a weaker currency while your costs are in a strong one squeezes you from both directions.
  • Judging remittances. The value of money sent home is not the converted figure but what it covers — school fees, rent, groceries. Senders who think in purchasing power send the right amount; senders who think only in their own currency often send too little or too much.
  • Comparing salaries across borders. A job offer abroad should be measured against the cost of living there, not converted and compared with home pay. Cost-of-living indexes exist precisely because the raw conversion lies.

Purchasing power at home: the inflation connection

Purchasing power is not only a travel concept. Inflation is purchasing power draining away domestically — the same banknote buying less each year — and over time the exchange rate tends to follow it down, as Inflation and Currency Value explains. The two ideas are one idea viewed from two sides: inside a country, prices measure what money buys; between countries, the exchange rate plus local prices measure the same thing.

Keep the two numbers separate: the exchange rate — check it on the converter — tells you what your money becomes. Purchasing power tells you what it is worth when you get there. Wise travellers, movers and earners check both.

Getting Paid in a Foreign Currency: A Practical Guide for Freelancers

Working for clients abroad is one of the great freedoms of modern freelancing — until the first payment arrives and a surprising slice of it is missing. Between invoice and bank account, foreign-currency income passes through exchange rates, margins and fees that employed people never see. This guide walks through each decision — what currency to invoice in, how to receive the money, and when to convert it — so the rate you negotiated is closer to the rate you keep.

Decision one: which currency goes on the invoice?

Many freelancers default to invoicing in the client's currency, because it feels professional and removes friction for the client. That is often right — but understand what it means: you have taken the exchange-rate risk onto yourself. Between the day you quote and the day you are paid, the rate can drift, and it drifts against your income, not the client's. The alternatives each shift the balance differently. Invoicing in your own currency makes your income predictable and pushes conversion costs to the client — reasonable for established freelancers with waiting lists, risky when competing for price-sensitive work. Invoicing in a major third currency such as US dollars or euros is the common middle path: both sides deal with a stable, liquid currency, spreads are tight, and if you also spend or save in that currency, you may not need to convert at all.

Whichever you choose, put the currency, the payment method and who bears conversion costs in the contract or the invoice notes. Ambiguity here is resolved later, by the payment system, at your expense.

Decision two: how the money reaches you

The receiving method determines most of your cost. An international wire into an ordinary domestic bank account is the traditional route and usually the most expensive: the sending client may pay a fee, intermediary banks may take deductions in transit, and your own bank will convert at its retail rate — typically one to four percent away from the mid-market rate, as explained in Why Banks Give You a Worse Exchange Rate Than Google. Multi-currency accounts offered by specialist providers have changed the maths for freelancers: they give you local account details in the client's country, so the client pays like a domestic transfer, the money arrives in the original currency, and you convert when you choose, at rates usually far closer to mid-market with a small visible fee. Payment platforms popular with clients are convenient but often the priciest of all once their conversion margin is counted — convenient for the client is not the same as cheap for you.

Whatever route you use, run the same test as any sender: what percentage of the mid-market value actually arrived? Our article on sending money abroad cheaply describes the comparison, and it works in reverse exactly.

Decision three: when to convert

If your account lets you hold foreign currency, you gain a choice most people never get: convert on your schedule, not the payment's. Practical approaches include converting on a fixed routine — say, moving everything to your home currency on payday — which removes emotion and decision fatigue; converting in tranches across the month to average the rate; or holding a balance in the foreign currency if you have genuine expenses in it, such as software subscriptions, subcontractors or travel, which eliminates conversion entirely for that share. What to avoid is the gambler's pattern: watching the rate daily, converting in a panic after a fall, and holding out for a recovery that may not come. As our article on timing exchanges explains, nobody predicts rates reliably — a boring routine beats a brilliant guess over a career.

Protecting the rate you actually earn

  • Build conversion costs into your pricing. If receiving foreign payments costs you two percent all-in, that is a business cost like software or tax — your rates should quietly include it.
  • Keep an exchange-rate buffer in quotes. For long projects paid on completion, quote with validity periods or a rate-adjustment clause, so a currency slide does not silently cut your fee.
  • Invoice promptly and chase politely. A late payment is not only late; it is extra weeks of rate exposure you never agreed to.
  • Keep records in both currencies. Note the amount invoiced, the amount received, the rate applied and the home-currency value on the day. Your tax obligations are usually calculated in your home currency, and reconstructing rates a year later is miserable — a two-line habit in a spreadsheet prevents it. (Tax rules differ by country; this is record-keeping advice, not tax advice.)

The freelancer's summary: choose the invoice currency deliberately, receive through an account that lets you hold and convert on your terms, convert on a routine rather than a hunch — and price the remaining cost into your rates, where it belongs.

How Central Banks Influence Exchange Rates

Central banks rarely admit to targeting an exchange rate — most officially aim at inflation and employment. Yet no institution moves currencies further or faster. Through the interest rates they set, the reserves they hold and occasionally a single well-chosen sentence, central banks shape the value of their money every day. Here is the toolkit, piece by piece.

Tool one: interest rates, the everyday lever

A central bank's policy rate sets the return on holding its currency in the safest possible form. Raise it, and savings, bonds and money-market holdings in that currency pay more; global investors shift funds toward it, and the buying pressure lifts the exchange rate. Cut it, and the flow reverses. Because markets trade on expectations, the movement usually happens before the decision — traders price in the expected path of rates months ahead, and it is the surprise in a decision, or in the language around it, that produces the sudden jumps. This mechanism is the single largest driver of day-to-day exchange rates among major currencies, and it is why the meetings of the US Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank and the Bank of England are marked on every currency trader's calendar. Its effects on the world's key currency are traced in What Affects the Value of the US Dollar?

Tool two: direct intervention in the market

Sometimes a central bank steps into the market as a trader itself. To strengthen its currency, it sells foreign reserves — dollars, typically — and buys its own money, directly adding demand. To weaken it, it does the reverse, selling its own currency and accumulating reserves. Intervention is most common among smaller and emerging-market central banks smoothing disorderly moves, and among countries managing a peg or a band, where defending the declared rate is the entire system — the mechanics described in Fixed vs Floating Exchange Rates. Large central banks intervene rarely, and usually in coordination with each other, because a lone intervention against the full weight of the global market tends to fade within days unless it signals a genuine policy shift behind it.

Tool three: reserves as both weapon and shield

The stockpile matters even when it is never used. A central bank holding deep foreign reserves can credibly promise to defend its currency, and that credibility alone deters speculative attacks — traders hesitate to bet against an opponent with very deep pockets. Thin reserves invite the opposite: doubts about how long a bank can hold its rate become self-fulfilling as money leaves early. This is why reserve figures are watched so closely in countries like Pakistan, where the State Bank's reserves act as the market's confidence gauge for the rupee, as our article on the USD to PKR rate explains.

Tool four: words — guidance and signals

Modern central banks have learned that language itself is policy. Through speeches, statements and press conferences, they guide expectations about future interest rates, and since exchange rates move on expectations, a hint can move a currency as surely as a decision. A governor describing inflation as "persistent" nudges markets to expect higher rates and lifts the currency before any vote is taken; a dovish phrase does the reverse. This "open mouth policy" costs nothing and commits to nothing, which is precisely its usefulness — and its limit, since credibility spent on hints that never materialise quickly runs out.

Tool five: the heavy machinery

In crises, central banks reach for bigger instruments: large-scale asset purchases that expand the money supply and tend to weigh on the currency, emergency lending that stabilises banks and stops panic-driven currency flight, and — at the extreme — capital controls that restrict money leaving the country altogether. Each trades long-term credibility for short-term calm, and each leaves a mark on the exchange rate that can last for years.

Why they usually deny caring about the rate

If central banks influence exchange rates so powerfully, why do most insist the rate is not their target? Partly diplomacy: openly chasing a weaker currency to help exporters invites retaliation and accusations of currency manipulation. Partly honesty: for large economies, the exchange rate is mainly a side effect of policy aimed at domestic goals — inflation first among them, as inflation and currency value sets out. The rate matters to them chiefly as a channel: a weaker currency makes imports dearer and can feed inflation, while a stronger one does the opposite. Understanding that chain — rates, inflation, currency, and back again — is most of what there is to understand about why your money's value abroad moves the way it does.

In short: central banks steer their currencies mainly by setting the price of holding them — the interest rate — backed by reserves, occasional direct trading, and words chosen with a trader's ear for their effect.

Currency Codes Explained: ISO 4217 and Why USD Means US Dollar

USD, EUR, PKR, JPY — every currency on this site, in your banking app and on every price board in the world carries the same three-letter code. Those codes are not abbreviations invented by banks for convenience. They come from a single international standard, ISO 4217, and once you know how the standard builds them, you can decode almost any currency you will ever meet — including the ones that break the pattern.

The basic recipe: country plus currency

Most ISO 4217 codes are built from two parts. The first two letters are the country code, taken from the companion standard ISO 3166: US for the United States, GB for Great Britain, JP for Japan, PK for Pakistan, IN for India. The third letter is usually the first letter of the currency's own name. Put them together and the system reads like a set of labelled drawers: US + D(ollar) = USD, GB + P(ound) = GBP, JP + Y(en) = JPY, PK + R(upee) = PKR, CH + F(ranc) = CHF — Switzerland using CH, its Latin-based country code, which is why the Swiss franc is not SWF. The pattern is so regular that you can often guess a code correctly on first sight, and — more usefully — read an unfamiliar code on a statement or price board and know immediately whose money it is.

Why codes exist at all: names and symbols are not enough

Currency names collide constantly. Dozens of countries call their money the dollar, the pound, the franc, the peso or the rupee — and the bare word "dollar" could mean the money of the United States, Canada, Australia, Singapore or Zimbabwe, all with different values. Symbols are worse: the $ sign serves many dollar and peso currencies, £ serves several pounds, and ¥ is shared by the Japanese yen and the Chinese yuan. A bank transferring money internationally cannot work with that ambiguity. The three-letter code is unambiguous, language-neutral and machine-friendly, which is why codes — not names or symbols — are what payment systems, converters and contracts actually use. On this site, the codes in the converter menus are ISO codes, and our article on reading currency pairs shows how they combine into quotes like EUR/USD.

The famous exception: EUR

The euro breaks the recipe because it has no single country. Its code, EUR, simply takes the first three letters of "Europe" — fitting for a currency shared by many nations, and a deliberate signal that it belongs to no one capital. It set the template for treating a currency union as its own entry in the standard. Other shared currencies follow the country-plus-name pattern of their region instead: the East Caribbean dollar is XCD, and the two CFA franc zones use XOF and XAF.

Codes beginning with X: the special drawer

That X is itself a rule of the standard. Codes starting with X denote currencies and instruments not tied to one country: XCD, XOF and XAF above; XDR, the International Monetary Fund's Special Drawing Rights, a basket unit used between central banks; and the codes for precious metals treated as money-like assets, such as XAU for gold and XAG for silver. If you meet an X code, you are looking at something supranational, shared or not-quite-a-currency — a useful instant clue when scanning a long list of rates.

More exceptions worth knowing

  • Historical renames. When a currency is redenominated — zeros cut after inflation, or a new unit issued — it gets a new code, so old and new money are never confused in records. Turkey's lira became TRY when the new lira replaced the old; Ghana's cedi became GHS at its redenomination.
  • The third letter is not always the name's initial. The Russian rouble is RUB and the Saudi riyal SAR, following the country prefix logic loosely rather than mechanically; the standard is a managed list, not a formula, and a committee maintains it.
  • Numeric codes exist too. Every currency also has a three-digit number — 840 for the US dollar, 978 for the euro, 586 for the Pakistani rupee — used in systems that prefer digits, such as card processing. You will rarely see them, but they are why a card terminal in any country can identify your currency without a single letter.
  • Funds and next-day codes. A few specialised codes distinguish the same currency settled on different terms for wholesale markets — a reminder that the standard serves traders as well as travellers.

Codes in everyday life

You already use these codes more than you think: in the currency menus of online shops, in the "pay in USD or EUR?" choice on international bookings, in the account details for receiving money from abroad — the freelancers in our guide to foreign-currency income live among them. Knowing the system turns a wall of three-letter abbreviations into a labelled map of the world's money, and it removes a small but real risk: confusing two currencies that share a name and symbol but not a value.

The decoder, memorised in ten seconds: two letters for the country, one for the currency's name — unless it starts with X, in which case it belongs to no single country at all.

What to Do With Leftover Travel Money

Almost every trip ends the same way: a handful of foreign notes and a jangle of coins at the bottom of a bag. Left in a drawer, that money quietly does nothing — and in an inflating world, slightly less than nothing each year. Here are your real options for leftover travel money, what each one costs, and how to come home with less of it next time.

Option one: keep it for the next trip

If there is a realistic chance you will return to the same country — or travel somewhere using the same currency, as with the euro across much of Europe — keeping the notes is often the smartest move of all. You avoid two conversions: you already paid the cost of buying the currency once, and exchanging it back and buying it again later would charge you the spread twice more. Store notes somewhere you will actually find them — with your passport is the classic — and treat them as the next trip's arrival cash, the small float our travel money guide recommends having for the first hours. The exception: if the destination's currency is inflating fast or you genuinely will not return for years, the money's slow erosion argues for converting it now.

Option two: exchange it back

Banks, post offices and bureaus will buy back major-currency notes, but understand the arithmetic: you are now on the other side of the spread. The buy-back rate is worse than the rate you bought at — that gap is the dealer's margin, charged on both journeys, which is why exchanging a currency round-trip typically costs several percent in total. A few practical rules soften the blow. Notes only: almost nobody buys back foreign coins at any price. Compare the buy-back rate against the mid-market rate on the converter exactly as you would when buying; some providers advertise special buy-back rates for customers who bought from them originally, occasionally including rate guarantees worth using if you kept the receipt. And shop around — buy-back margins vary as widely as selling margins do.

Option three: spend it before you leave

The cheapest conversion is the one that never happens. In the last day of a trip, leftover cash can absorb the hotel bill (ask to pay the cash portion in local currency rather than by card), the airport transfer, meals, gifts and the souvenirs you were going to buy anyway. At the airport itself, remaining notes can be spent in shops — prices are rarely kind, but a useful purchase at a mediocre price beats a note exchanged at a bad rate and then spent at home anyway. One warning: if you pay partly in cash and partly by card at a hotel, make sure the card portion is charged in the local currency, not converted for you — the dynamic currency conversion trap applies to final bills too.

Option four: give it away, usefully

Small amounts — especially coins, which have almost no exchange value anywhere — can do genuine good. Many airlines collect spare foreign change on board for children's charities, airports run donation globes for local causes, and international charities accept foreign currency donations by post, coins included, aggregating millions of travellers' leftovers into real funding. Friends, family or colleagues heading to the same destination will usually happily swap at the mid-market rate — the rare exchange where both sides win, since neither pays a spread. And a single note kept deliberately, as a souvenir, is not waste at all; it is the cheapest souvenir you brought home.

The coins problem, specifically

Coins deserve their own rule because they behave differently from notes: dealers refuse them, banks that accept them do so at punitive rates, and they accumulate trip after trip. Spend coins first throughout the trip — they are perfect for buses, tips, coffee and market stalls — and make a point of emptying them into the last day's spending. Whatever survives the flight home is donation-globe money, not savings.

Next time: come home with less

  • Spend cash down deliberately in the final two days, shifting remaining expenses onto your no-fee card.
  • Withdraw smaller amounts more carefully near the end of the trip — the last ATM withdrawal is the one that becomes leftovers.
  • Keep a rough daily spend figure in mind so the end of the trip is planned, not discovered.
  • Remember that unspent money on a travel-money card can usually be converted back in the app — check its buy-back rate against the market before accepting it.

The leftover hierarchy: keep it if you will return, spend it if you can, swap it with a friend if possible, exchange notes at a compared rate if you must — and let the coins go to a good cause.
